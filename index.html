        Prologue

        Every street follows small rules

        The bumps under your feet at a crossing, the armrest in the middle of a bench, the language on a street sign. Nobody reads them on purpose, but they shape how you move and who feels welcome.

        These photographs come from slow walks through Wales, London, New York and Boston. Old photographs, maps and satellite views show how each street came to look the way it does. Psychogeography is the habit of noticing all this: how the shape of a place changes the way you feel in it.

        1940

        One year keeps coming up. In September 1940 a German bomb blew the front off the Queen's Theatre on Shaftesbury Avenue, which is why Les Misérables now plays behind a modern glass corner. Wartime bombing also wrecked Limehouse, London's first Chinatown, and its people moved to Soho. That August a bomb damaged the chantry priest's house at St Illtud's in Llantwit Major. The war shaped streets from Wales to the West End.

        A dérive through Llantwit Major

        23 March 2026 · one hour on foot

        A dérive is a walk with no destination: you follow whatever pulls at you and see where the town takes you. The Situationists invented it in 1950s Paris to notice how a city's layout steers people's moods.

        I walked out from the station and photographed whatever stopped me. Each dot is a photo, placed by my phone's GPS and joined in the order I took them. Tap a dot to see it.

        Underfoot

        The ground talks to people who can't see it.

        Cardiff city centre ChapterI

        A language for feet

        Tactile paving

        Seiichi Miyake invented tactile paving in Japan in 1965, inspired by braille. The first blocks went down on a street in Okayama in 1967. The UK, the US and Australia adopted their own versions in the early 1990s, and each country wrote its own grammar.

        Britain's is the most detailed. Round blisters mean “a crossing is here”, red where traffic is controlled (as at a zebra) and buff where it isn't. Bars across your path (“corduroy”) warn of steps or a hazard. Bars running along your path are a guidance route, used where there's no kerb or wall to follow, as in a pedestrianised shopping street.

        So the red pad at the Llantwit Major zebra and the buff strip down Cardiff's shopping street are two different words in the same language. The Cardiff strip also shows a rule being broken: a steel bollard stands right in the middle of the route it's meant to keep clear.

        Four countries, four grammars

        UK · United States · Japan · China

        The same idea took a different shape in each country, depending on who wrote the rules and how fast the paving went down.

        Why are they so different?

        Who writes the rules. Britain's Department for Transport publishes detailed national guidance, so every shape and colour has one meaning everywhere. In the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 made one symbol, the “detectable warning”, compulsory at kerb ramps and platform edges, and left guidance routes to local choice. Japan's rules were standardised in 2001, and since 1994 large buildings have had to install the blocks.

        How fast it was built. Japan added tactile paving gradually over decades. China adopted the same yellow two-shape system and built at enormous speed: since 2001 major streets have been required to have “blind lanes”, and Beijing alone laid more than 1,600 km. Building fast without consulting blind users led to lanes that run into trees, holes, poles and parked bikes. In 2023 China passed a national law on barrier-free environments, which covers keeping facilities such as blind lanes clear.

        What the street is for. In car-centred American towns the danger point is the kerb, so the domes cluster there. In Japanese and Chinese cities, where huge numbers of people walk to stations, long guidance routes lead from the street right onto the platform.

        Above the paving: Belisha beacons and Welsh signs

        The black-and-white pole at the Llantwit Major crossing is a Belisha beacon, named after Leslie Hore-Belisha, the Minister of Transport who added the amber globes to crossings in 1934. The stripes painted on the road came later and gave the zebra crossing its name.

        Up the road, the footpath disc says Public Footpath and Llwybr Cyhoeddus. Bilingual signs in Wales were won by Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg, the Welsh Language Society, which campaigned against English-only road signs from the late 1960s, often by painting over them.

        City plans

        Grid versus growth

        Why American streets look so different

        Walk in Wales and the lanes bend around old field edges, churches and streams. Walk in most American towns and you turn at right angles every block. The difference is easiest to see from space.

        Sold by the square mile

        The Land Ordinance of 1785

        After independence, the United States had a huge territory to sell. It was surveyed into townships six miles square, each split into 36 one-mile sections, and roads followed the section lines.

        In most states west of Pennsylvania you can drive a mile, turn ninety degrees and drive another mile. The farms of the Vale of Glamorgan were never surveyed that way; their hedges follow ownership, streams and old tracks.

        An island laid flat

        The Commissioners' Plan of 1811

        New York's commissioners laid 12 avenues and 155 numbered streets over Manhattan, about 2,000 blocks, tilted 29 degrees to fit the island.

        They argued that straight streets and square houses were the cheapest to build and the most convenient to live in, and equal lots were easy to sell. From space you can still see where the grid begins and the older, tangled streets of lower Manhattan, including Chinatown, end.

        Then came the car

        Zoning, suburbs and the Interstate

        A 1926 Supreme Court case, Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty, made zoning legal, and American towns began separating homes, shops and factories, so most errands needed a drive.

        After the Second World War, suburbs like Levittown filled with curving streets of near-identical houses. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 funded 41,000 miles of Interstate, and some of it was driven straight through city neighbourhoods. Boston's Chinatown was squeezed between the elevated Central Artery and the Massachusetts Turnpike.

        Britain went another way. Most British towns were built before cars, around medieval lanes and market streets like Llantwit Major's. After the war, the Town and Country Planning Act 1947 meant you needed planning permission to build almost anything, and it started the system of protecting historic buildings. Old street patterns survived because the law made them hard to change.

        Timeline: how the American street was made
        1. 1785Land Ordinance: the West is surveyed into six-mile townships and one-mile sections.
        2. 1811The Commissioners' Plan lays the grid over Manhattan. It takes about 60 years to reach 155th Street.
        3. 1926Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty: the Supreme Court upholds zoning.
        4. 1947Levittown, New York, begins: mass-produced houses for returning soldiers. In Britain, the Town and Country Planning Act is passed the same year.
        5. 1950sBoston's elevated Central Artery is built through downtown, beside Chinatown. It was later replaced by a tunnel in the Big Dig, finished in 2007.
        6. 1956The Federal-Aid Highway Act funds the 41,000-mile Interstate system.

        Built for whom

        You may sit. You may not stay.

        Cardiff city centre ChapterIII

        Defensive design

        Benches, studs and perch seats

        Some street furniture is designed to stop people as much as to help them. Planners call it defensive design. Critics call it hostile architecture.

        Armrests divide a bench so no one can lie down. A steel stud on a planter edge stops skateboards from grinding. Perch seats at a bus stop are tipped forward so you lean instead of resting. Each one is a small decision about who the street is for, and how long they're allowed to stay.

        A short history of hostile design
        1. 1800sVictorian cities fit angled “urine deflectors” into dark corners and doorways.
        2. 2012Camden council in London installs the Camden bench, a lumpy concrete block designed to resist sleeping, skateboarding, graffiti and even drug dealing.
        3. 2014Photos of metal spikes outside a London block of flats go viral. After protests, the spikes are taken out.
        4. 2016In Liverpool, a sloped steel cover is put over steps at a former Bank of England building to stop people sleeping there.

        Supporters say these designs keep public space orderly. Critics say they push away homeless people and make streets less welcoming for everyone. A 2020 review noted there was still no large study of how they affect homeless people's wellbeing.

        Three Chinatowns, three ways in.

        Chinatown, London ChapterIV

        One name, three places

        New York · London · Boston

        “Chinatown” sounds like one kind of place, but each one shows who arrived, when, and who the street now serves.

        In London you enter through a gate under a ceiling of red lanterns, and the council's own street signs are in Chinese. In Manhattan there's no gate: tenement fire escapes, a neon welcome sign and political flags do the talking. Boston has a classic gate, and the flags hanging under it say as much as the inscription above.

        Mott Street, New York

        Since 1858

        Chinatown grew up here as a “bachelor society”: the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 stopped most immigration, so men couldn't bring their families for decades.

        The fire escapes and the two scripts on Mott Street's signs are read up close in chapter VI.

        Timeline: Manhattan's Chinatown
        1. 1858Ah Ken, often named as the first Chinese person to settle permanently in the area, arrives.
        2. 1872Wo Kee opens a general store on Mott Street. Most early migrants are men from Taishan in Guangdong.
        3. 1882The Chinese Exclusion Act stops most Chinese immigration.
        4. 1883The Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association (CCBA) is founded. It acts almost like a local government.
        5. 1899–1911Kang Youwei's Chinese Empire Reform Association, which wants an emperor under a constitution, runs its New York base on Mott Street. Sun Yat-sen's revolutionaries compete for the same people's money and loyalty.
        6. 1930s–70sKuomintang (Nationalist) loyalists take over community organisations and hold the CCBA chair from 1948 into the 1970s.
        7. 1962The CCBA opens its “China Tower” at 62 Mott Street, which still flies the ROC flag beside the Stars and Stripes.
        8. 1960s onNew migrants from Hong Kong and Taiwan arrive, and Cantonese becomes the main language.

        Gerrard Street, London

        A Chinatown that moved

        London's first Chinatown was in Limehouse, by the docks. After wartime bombing, it moved about 7 km west to the cheap, run-down streets behind the theatres of Soho.

        Timeline: London's Chinatown
        1. 1677–85Gerrard Street is built on the Earl of Macclesfield's military training ground. Later residents include John Dryden.
        2. 1880s–1930sLondon's first Chinatown grows in Limehouse in the East End, serving sailors at the docks.
        3. 1898–99Charles Booth's poverty map colours Gerrard Street as “fairly comfortable” to “mixed”. No Chinese businesses yet.
        4. 1940–45Wartime bombing devastates Limehouse.
        5. 1950sThe first Chinese restaurants open on Lisle Street, where rents are cheap and West End diners are close by.
        6. 1960s–70sMigrants from rural Hong Kong arrive. Gerrard Street becomes the centre of Chinese London.
        7. 1980sThe London Chinatown Community Centre opens in 1980, a Chinese-style pavilion goes up in Newport Place, and the first two gates are finished in 1986.
        8. 2016A third gate, in the Qing dynasty style, is made by craftspeople in China, assembled in London and unveiled on Wardour Street by the Duke of York and China's ambassador.

        Beach Street, Boston

        A gate from Taiwan

        Boston's Chinese community began with workers brought east to break a strike in 1870. A century later, highways cut through the neighbourhood, and in 1982 Taiwan's government offered the city a gate.

        The gate stands at the corner of Beach Street and Surface Road, on a site that once held a ventilation building for the Central Artery tunnel; during the Big Dig it became a garden. Its inscription, flags and lions are read up close in chapter VI.

        Timeline: Boston's Chinatown
        1. 1870Chinese workers are brought from San Francisco to break a shoe-factory strike in North Adams, Massachusetts. Many later move to Boston.
        2. 1875Hong Far Low, the first restaurant, opens.
        3. 1903A police raid arrests 234 people in Chinatown, and 45 are later deported.
        4. 1950s–60sThe Central Artery and the Massachusetts Turnpike take homes, the library and business land.
        5. 1982Taiwan's government offers the city the gate at Beach Street.

        Why the flag flies in America

        And not in London

        The blue-and-red flag in the New York and Boston photos is the flag of the Republic of China, now based in Taiwan.

        In 1911 overseas Chinese communities in America backed the revolution that founded the Republic, and many of their associations have flown its flag ever since. During the Second World War the US fought alongside the Nationalists, and Chinese American veterans came home loyal to them. After the Communists won the civil war in 1949, Nationalist supporters ran key associations such as New York's CCBA, and the US kept recognising the ROC as “China” until 1979. Many older families also had bitter memories of the Communist Party, so the flags stayed up after Washington's switch. Boston's gate says the same thing in stone.

        London's story runs the other way. Britain recognised the People's Republic on 6 January 1950, partly to protect Hong Kong, then a British colony. Most of the people who built Gerrard Street came from Hong Kong, not as political exiles, so their Chinatown is decorated with lanterns, lions and council street signs rather than party flags. The official presence there is Beijing's and Hong Kong's: China's ambassador helped unveil the newest gate in 2016, and the festival banner on the Gerrard Street gate in chapter VI carries the logos of China's tourist office in London and Hong Kong's trade office.

        The same calendar everywhere

        Lunar New Year

        At Lunar New Year, lion dancers move from shop to shop to bring good luck, drums and cymbals clear the street, and red envelopes of “lucky money” change hands.

        The red lanterns strung over Gerrard Street are New Year decorations that have become part of the street itself. In New York, photographers were recording the same celebrations on Mott Street more than a century ago.

        Wales has a Chinese story too

        Tiger Bay, 1911

        Cardiff's docklands, later called Tiger Bay, were home to sailors from all over the world, including Chinese seamen.

        During the national seamen's strike in the summer of 1911, shipowners hired Chinese sailors to keep the port running. They were attacked in the street, and the violence spread to the homes and businesses of Chinese people living in Cardiff.

        Theatreland

        Two twins, one bomb.

        Shaftesbury Avenue, London · number plates blurred ChapterV

        A street cut for traffic

        Shaftesbury Avenue, 1877–86

        The architect George Vulliamy and the engineer Joseph Bazalgette cut Shaftesbury Avenue through the crowded slums of St Giles and Soho as a new route for traffic. Its cheap, wide plots on a main road suited theatres.

        The Lyric, where Hadestown plays, opened in 1888 and is the oldest theatre still standing on the avenue. Chinatown's Gerrard Street runs right behind it; its first restaurants fed post-show crowds, which helps explain why London's Chinatown faces visitors more than New York's does.

        Close readings

        A city of arcades

        Cardiff, 1858–1902

        Cardiff calls itself the City of Arcades: it has the highest concentration of Victorian and Edwardian shopping arcades of any British city. An arcade is a street with a roof on it, lined with small shops and lit from above.

        The first, the Royal Arcade, opened in 1858, when Cardiff was only beginning to boom as a coal port. Six more followed in the next 44 years, and every one is now a listed building. Their roofs borrowed from the train sheds of the day, and their shopfronts stayed small, so they still feel like the streets of a much older town tucked inside the modern centre.

        The Castle Arcade of 1887 is listed as the finest. It turns a corner on its way from the High Street to the castle, its upper floors are wooden galleries, and footbridges with ivy-leaf ironwork cross the passage at first-floor level. Tap the numbers to read the photo.

        The department store's street

        Morgan Arcade, 1896–99

        David Morgan's department store built this arcade to link its two entrances, on St Mary Street and The Hayes. It opened in 1899 as the New Central Arcade.

        Some of its first-floor Venetian windows survive, and so do original wooden shopfronts: slender frames that are almost all glass, so the goods do the talking. One of them now belongs to Spillers Records, founded in 1894 and recognised as the oldest record shop in the world.

        A gate that says “enter”

        The paifang, or Chinatown arch

        A paifang is a traditional Chinese gateway. In ancient Chinese cities each walled ward had gates that were shut and guarded every night. Over the centuries the gate became purely decorative, and abroad it became the sign for “Chinatown”.

        The classic parts are posts, tiled roofs with upturned eaves and small beasts on the ridges, and a central board of calligraphy. The first one in the United States was a temporary gate at San Francisco's 1915 world's fair; the oldest still standing is Los Angeles' West Gate of 1938. Washington's Friendship Archway (1986) is one of the biggest, and Liverpool's (2000) is the largest in Europe.

        London's and Boston's gates look alike, but read what hangs on them and they tell opposite political stories.

        Stairs on the outside

        New York's tenements

        The zigzag iron stairs on Mott Street are there by law. New York's Tenement House Act of 1867 required a fire escape from every apartment, and iron balconies and ladders bolted to the street front became the usual answer.

        Later laws went after light and air. The 1879 act said every room needed a window to the open air, which produced narrow “dumbbell” tenements with air shafts between buildings; the 1901 act replaced the shafts with proper courtyards. The fire escapes became extra rooms, for plants, bikes and, on hot summer nights, sleeping. In Rear Window a couple drags their mattress onto one; in West Side Story it's where Tony and Maria meet.

        The red box

        The K6 telephone kiosk

        Sir Giles Gilbert Scott designed the K6 in 1935 for George V's Silver Jubilee, as a smaller, cheaper version of his earlier K2. It was the first red box used widely outside London, and public telephones in Britain rose from 19,000 in 1935 to 35,000 in 1940.

        About 2,000 old phone boxes are now listed buildings. Many no longer hold a phone at all: under BT's Adopt a Kiosk scheme, communities have turned them into tiny libraries and homes for defibrillators.

        Plaques

        Who gets to write the history?

        Llantwit Major ChapterVII

        The authority of blue

        Since 1866

        Blue plaques began in London in 1866, when the Society of Arts started marking the homes of famous people. The first, for Lord Byron, went up in 1867.

        The official London scheme, now run by English Heritage, says a person must have been dead for 20 years, which keeps it slow and careful. The idea spread everywhere. Local societies, councils, the BBC and private trusts now put up plaques in the same blue, and the colour carries the authority whoever paid for it.

        The Llantwit Major Local History Society marks the Court House and St Illtud's Church, which grew from a monastic college founded in the early 500s and is sometimes called the “Westminster Abbey of Wales”. The David Bowie plaque isn't English Heritage: BBC Music Day and the British Plaque Trust unveiled it in 2017 outside Trident Studios in Soho, where he recorded Hunky Dory and Ziggy Stardust, only a year after he died.

        Nothing happened here

        Invented memory

        The “On this site Sept. 5, 1782 nothing happened” plaque is a mass-produced joke. The exact same words are on a guesthouse in Malvern.

        A notice on a lamppost from a made-up “Bureau of Urban Re-cooptation” reclassifies a streetlight as a “Nocturnal Observation Post”. Both jokes work because we've learned to trust anything that looks official.

        St Illtud's Church in seven dates
        1. Early 500sIlltud founds a monastic college here, one of the earliest centres of learning in Britain.
        2. 800sCarved standing stones and Celtic crosses are set up on the site. They're now inside the West Church.
        3. After 1066The Normans give the church's income to Tewkesbury Abbey and build a new church.
        4. 1200sA tower and a large monastic farm are added. The East Church is built for the farm community.
        5. c. 1400The East Church gets aisles and a taller tower. The Galilee chapel is added to the West Church.
        6. c. 1500The Raglan family endows the Galilee chapel as a chantry.

        Cardiff ChapterVIII

        The street's comments section

        Chalk, stickers, tape

        Between the official signs, people leave their own: chalk on a shed, stickers on a bin, a drawing taped to a utility box, a card in a church asking you to hold a prayer as you leave.

        Even the clothes bank speaks two languages. Welsh is spoken by about one in six people in Wales, and it appears on everything from road signs to charity bins, next to the red dragon of the Welsh flag.

        Queen Street, 120 years apart

        Cardiff

        In 1905 the messages were painted shop signs and awnings, and the street belonged to trams and carts. Today it's pedestrianised, and the loudest message is a digital billboard.
